The person under the microscope

A researcher, a geologist, a poet, a photographer — a serious person who admires the comedy of life.

I grew up in Mughalsarai, Uttar Pradesh, and finished school there. Delhi came next — a B.Sc. (Hons) in Geology at Hansraj College, then an M.Sc. at the University of Delhi. The Ph.D. followed at IIT Kharagpur, in Earth and Planetary Science, where I am now an Institute Postdoctoral Fellow in the Experimental Petrology Lab.

My work is question-driven, not technique-driven. The instruments — FIB-TEM, FEG-SEM, FE-EPMA, Raman — are how I get an answer, never the reason I ask. What I actually want to know is what happens to a mineral in the few hundred milliseconds when an asteroid hits another asteroid, and what that tells us about the deep Earth we cannot reach.

"Meteorites are leftovers from the formation of the solar system." They kept the original chemistry that Earth spent four and a half billion years destroying.

Longer term, I want to build an independent shock-metamorphism and high-pressure planetary mineralogy group in India — one wired directly into ISRO's planetary mission roadmap. Indian falls like Katol, Kamargaon and Bori are world-class samples. They should be studied here.

Why the gold bar

The monogram is KT twice over: my initials, and the K–Pg boundary. The golden bar is that iridium-rich clay layer — 66 million years of ordinary rock, interrupted by one afternoon. The asteroid at the top is the cause, and the reason I have a job.

What a shock-melt vein is

Two asteroids meet. A shock wave crosses the rock in microseconds. Along fractures, friction spikes the temperature and the rock melts in place — a thin dark vein, quenched before it can forget.

Inside those veins, olivine, pyroxene and feldspar are pushed past the point where their ordinary crystal structures can survive. They collapse into high-pressure polymorphs — denser packings of the same atoms, the same minerals that make up Earth's transition zone and lower mantle.

That is the trick. A meteorite you can hold in one hand contains, in a vein a hundred microns wide, the mineralogy of a region 660 km beneath your feet that no drill will ever reach.

Identify the phases, measure their compositions, model how fast they grew, and you can back out the pressure, the temperature and the duration of an impact that happened before there were oceans.

My samples are mostly Indian falls — Katol (L6), Kamargaon (L6) and Bori (L6) — plus impact-melt bombs from the Lonar crater in Maharashtra, the only well-preserved terrestrial crater in basalt.

The transformation sequence I look for

Olivine
(Mg,Fe)₂SiO₄ · < 410 km

The starting material. Ordinary chondrite bulk mineralogy, unshocked and orthorhombic.

Wadsleyite
β-phase · ~410–520 km

First collapse. Same formula, denser packing. Marks the top of the transition zone.

Ringwoodite
γ-spinel · ~520–660 km

Deep blue, spinel-structured. In Katol it grew from a superheated chondritic melt — my 2025 paper.

Bridgmanite
(Mg,Fe,Al)SiO₃ · > 660 km

Earth's most abundant mineral. We found natural Fe-bearing aluminous bridgmanite in Katol — PNAS, 2021.
